## Description

```
In 1814, the governor of a struggling colony on the Red River made it illegal to carry one food out of his territory. Two and a half years later, more than twenty men were dead at Frog Plain. The food was pemmican: dried, pounded bison meat sealed in rendered fat and sewn into a rawhide bag. It was a Plains Indigenous technology that the fur trade could not run without.

This documentary follows pemmican from the Cree and Assiniboine camps that made it, along the North West Company's 3,000-mile canoe road, to Miles Macdonell's order of 8 January 1814, the seizure of 479 bags on the Souris River, the killings of 19 June 1816 (the Seven Oaks Massacre in settler memory, the Victory of Frog Plain for the Métis), and the collapse of the bison herds that ended the pemmican economy. It closes with the bison's return and the Buffalo Treaty of 2014.

HOW THIS FILM WAS MADE (please read)
- Narration is a synthetic voice (Kokoro-82M, open-source TTS). It is not a human recording.
- All 3D scenes are original Blender reconstructions. They are illustrative, not historical records or photographs: no specific camp, fort or battlefield layout is shown as documented fact.
- The order of 8 January 1814 and the glossary and title pages shown on screen are real pages of an 1819 printed book, *Report of the proceedings connected with the disputes between the Earl of Selkirk and the North-West Company … York, Upper Canada, October 1818* (Montreal: Lane and Mower, 1819; University of Toronto copy, Internet Archive, public domain). The yellow highlighting was added for this film. The printed text of the order differs slightly between 1819 printings; the film follows this one.
- Paintings and historical photographs: Smithsonian Institution Open Access (CC0). George Catlin's paintings from the upper Missouri, 1832–39, and his Comanche village and Sault Ste. Marie canoe race (Smithsonian American Art Museum); Eadweard Muybridge, "Buffalo gallop", 1884–86 (National Museum of American History); William T. Hornaday and his 1887 bison group, and the National Zoo herd about 1898 (Smithsonian Institution Archives); a National Zoo bison, 2014. Also from the Library of Congress (no known restrictions on publication): B. F. Upton's photograph of Red River carts (Minnesota, 1867–75), two 1874 Harper's Weekly engravings by Frenzeny & Tavernier of the hide trade, a photograph of the nucleus of a bison herd (1900–20), and three 1860 photographs of Hudson's Bay Company posts on the Columbia River (Fort Vancouver and Fort Colville), shown as examples of such posts and captioned with their own place and year. Each is credited on screen while shown. The paintings show the bison-hunting world of the Plains nations in the 1830s, not Red River in 1814.
- Maps: Natural Earth (public domain), NASA/NOAA shaded relief (public domain). Boundaries, homelands and routes are approximate and labelled as such.
```
