P01 | This bag weighs about ninety pounds. It's rawhide, sewn shut, and the seams were sealed with grease. Inside is food. Packed like this and kept dry, it could last for years.

P02 | Two hundred years ago, bags like this crossed the northern half of a continent by canoe. And on the eighth of January, 1814, the governor of a small, hungry colony on the Red River made it illegal to carry them out of his territory.

P03 | Not illegal to eat. Illegal to move.

P04 | Two and a half years later, more than twenty men lay dead on a plain just downriver from his fort. It was the bloodiest evening of what historians now call the Pemmican War.

P05 | The food in the bag is pemmican. To understand why people would fight over it, you have to understand the problem it solved, and who knew how to make it.

P06 | Fresh meat is mostly water. That's what makes it heavy, and that's what makes it rot. On the northern plains, a good bison hunt could bring in far more meat than anyone could eat before it spoiled. The hard part was never the hunt. It was keeping what the hunt provided.

P07 | The fur traders didn't solve that problem. They learned the solution from the people who already had it. Plains peoples, among them the Cree and the Assiniboine, whose lands met the fur trade along these rivers, made a food that could travel. The English word comes from Cree: pimîhkân, built on the word for fat, or grease.

P08 | The lean meat was cut into thin sheets and dried, in the sun and wind or over a slow fire, until it was hard and brittle. Then it was pounded, often on a spread hide, until it broke down into something close to coarse meal. Fat from the same animal was rendered, and the hot fat was poured over the pounded meat and worked all the way through it. Sometimes dried fruit went in, saskatoon berries or chokecherries. The mixture was packed into a rawhide bag while it was still warm, and the bag was sewn shut.

P09 | Most of that work was done by women. Their skill decided whether a summer's hunt became a year of food, or a pile of spoiled meat.

P10 | Every step had a job. Drying removed the water that bacteria and mold need to grow. It took five or six pounds of fresh, lean meat to make a single pound of dried, pounded meat, so a heavy load became a light one.

P11 | The fat did two things. It added energy, more than twice as much per ounce as the meat itself. And as it cooled, it locked the fibers in place and kept out air and moisture. The bag, greased along its seams, sealed the whole package.

P12 | None of this made pemmican indestructible. Pemmican could go rancid, and damp could ruin it. But packed well and kept dry, a bag could last for years. What came out was dense, durable food that could be eaten cold, straight from the bag, by someone who had no time to stop and cook.

P13 | And at the start of the eighteen-hundreds, there was an entire industry made of people who had no time to stop.

P14 | The North West Company, run by merchants in Montreal, wanted the finest furs on the continent, and some of the best came from the Athabasca country in the far northwest. The canoe route there ran close to three thousand miles. It was so long that the company split the journey in two, with crews from Montreal and crews from the interior meeting halfway, at the western end of Lake Superior.

P15 | The northern season was short. Canoes had to get in and get out before the rivers froze. Every pound of food on board was a pound of trade goods or furs that couldn't be carried. And a crew that stopped to hunt was a crew that wasn't paddling.

P16 | Pemmican changed that math. Fur-trade accounts reckoned that around two pounds a day could keep a working man going. So the company built a supply chain around it. Pemmican was made on the plains, bought from Indigenous and Métis hunters, and stockpiled at depots along the route. A northern canoe with a crew of six, making perhaps thirty-five miles a day, would take on about four ninety-pound bags at one depot. That was enough to carry the crew to the next depot, hundreds of miles away.

P17 | A large share of that pemmican came from the Métis, a people whose roots lay in the fur trade itself, in families formed by traders and Cree and Ojibwe women. By the early eighteen-hundreds, Métis communities along the Red and Assiniboine rivers had their own customs and their own economy. Each year, whole families went out onto the plains together for the hunt, organized almost like a moving town, with elected captains and strict rules. What they brought back, dried and packed, was sold to the posts.

P18 | Historians who study this trade put it plainly: without cheap, abundant pemmican from the plains, the fur trade of the far northwest would not have been possible. The furs came from the north. The fuel came from bison country.

P19 | The two great fur companies weren't equally exposed. The Hudson's Bay Company could ship goods by sea straight into the heart of the continent, through Hudson Bay. The North West Company had no such shortcut. Its lifeline was the long canoe road from Montreal, and that road ran on pemmican. Much of that pemmican came down rivers that met at one place: the point where the Assiniboine River joins the Red.

P20 | In 1811, the Hudson's Bay Company granted a Scottish aristocrat, the Earl of Selkirk, a tract of land of about a hundred and sixteen thousand square miles. That's roughly the size of Italy. Selkirk and his relatives had bought heavily into the company, and he meant to fill this land with farmers, many of them from the Scottish Highlands. He called it Assiniboia. Right in the middle of it sat the junction of those two rivers, and the North West Company's supply line.

P21 | The first settlers arrived in 1812, with a governor named Miles Macdonell. Farming at Red River got off to a hard start, and the colony leaned on bison meat it could hunt or buy. By the winter of 1813, food was short, and more families were on the way.

P22 | On January eighth, 1814, Macdonell issued his order. For one year, no provisions gathered in Assiniboia could be taken out of it. Not meat, not fish, not grain, not vegetables. Traders could keep what they needed to reach their own posts, with a license from the governor. Anything else could be seized, and would be paid for.

P23 | On paper, it was about feeding a colony. In practice, it landed on the one product the North West Company could not do without, and on the Métis families whose living came from making and selling it. To them, it looked less like famine relief than an attempt to control their country and their trade. Both of those things could be true at once.

P24 | That spring, the colony enforced it. A sheriff named John Spencer was sent to a North West Company post on the Souris River. His party took four hundred and seventy-nine bags of pemmican, along with kegs of fat and bundles of dried meat. That's more than forty thousand pounds of food, taken from a company that needed it to move its canoes north.

P25 | The North West Company answered. Over the next year, its people worked to break the colony apart, and in June of 1815, most of the settlers left. Macdonell himself was taken east under arrest. For a moment, the colony was gone.

P26 | It didn't stay gone. Settlers returned that same year, and a new governor, Robert Semple, arrived in the fall. In March of 1816, the colony's men seized the North West Company's post at the Forks, Fort Gibraltar, and later pulled it down.

P27 | By then, the Métis had a leader of their own: Cuthbert Grant, barely in his twenties, the son of a North West Company partner and a Métis mother. That spring, Grant's men seized Hudson's Bay Company pemmican on the Qu'Appelle River, hundreds of bags of it. Pemmican was now both the prize and the weapon.

P28 | In June, Grant's party, around sixty riders, Métis and First Nations, set out to carry pemmican east to meet the North West Company's canoes on Lake Winnipeg. To get there, they had to pass the colony. They cut across country, over a stretch of low ground the Métis called la Grenouillère. Frog Plain.

P29 | On the evening of June nineteenth, Governor Semple walked out from Fort Douglas with about two dozen men to stop them.

P30 | What happened next has been argued over for two centuries. Words were exchanged. Someone fired. Each side said the other fired first. Within minutes, Semple and twenty of his men were dead. On the Métis side, at least one young man was killed.

P31 | Grant's men went on to take Fort Douglas, and the remaining settlers were forced to leave. For the second time in a little over a year, the colony was emptied.

P32 | In the settler tradition, the killings became the Seven Oaks Massacre. For the Métis, it was the Victory of Frog Plain, la Victoire de la Grenouillère. A Métis songwriter named Pierre Falcon made a song about it that same year, and it was sung for generations. Many historians, and many Métis people today, see these years as a moment when the Métis spoke of themselves as a nation.

P33 | The killing settled nothing quickly. Lord Selkirk answered with force of his own, seizing the North West Company's inland headquarters at Fort William that August. Lawsuits and inquiries dragged on for years. Finally, under pressure from the British government, the two rivals stopped fighting, and in 1821 they merged under the Hudson's Bay Company name.

P34 | And the pemmican kept moving. If anything, the trade grew. In 1840, a single summer hunt out of Red River set out with more than twelve hundred carts. Later in the century, factory-made pemmican went to the ends of the earth. In 1911, Roald Amundsen's team carried it to the South Pole, in a version that added oatmeal and dried peas.

P35 | Which leaves the question that pemmican still raises. A food this compact, this durable, this proven. Why did it fade from everyday life?

P36 | Part of the answer is canned food and refrigeration. But on the plains, the deeper answer is simpler, and harder. The animal it was made from nearly disappeared.

P37 | Nobody knows exactly how many bison once lived on the plains. Estimates run into the tens of millions. Historians argue that a century of feeding the fur trade had already worn the northern herds down. Then, in the 1870s, new tanning methods turned bison hides into industrial leather, and commercial hunters killed the animals by the millions.

P38 | By 1879, the bison were essentially gone from the Canadian plains. Even the government's own commissioner in the region admitted the disappearance had taken Ottawa by surprise. Hunger followed, for the nations whose lives had been built around the herds.

P39 | In 1889, the naturalist William Hornaday tried to count every bison left in North America. His total was one thousand and ninety-one.

P40 | The pemmican economy didn't end because the idea failed. It ended because the world that produced it was taken apart.

P41 | Bison have slowly come back since then, and pemmican never truly vanished. It's still made in Indigenous kitchens, by people who learned it the way it was always learned: from someone who knew how. And in 2014, nations from both sides of the border, among them the Blackfeet, the Blood Tribe, the Siksika and the Piikani, signed what they call the Buffalo Treaty, a pact to bring the animal back to their lands. It was the first treaty among them in more than a hundred and fifty years.

P42 | Look at the bag again. A governor tried to keep it in one valley. A war was fought around it. Explorers carried it to the bottom of the world. But the knowledge inside it never belonged to the colony, or to the companies. It belonged to the people who learned how to turn one short season of plenty into a year of survival.
